What to charge to install a water heater

The honest answer is a range — and where you land in it says more about your pricing than about your plumbing.

The Markup · Dead Hand

It's the most-quoted job in the trade, which is exactly why so many shops get it wrong. A water heater feels like a known number — you've done a hundred of them — so it's the first job people quote from memory, undercharge, and never notice the leak in their own margin. Let's put a real number on it, then show you how to build your own instead of borrowing anyone's.

What actually drives the number

Before you can charge for it, you have to see everything the price is carrying. On a water heater, that's the unit itself (tank or tankless, gas or electric, the size), the labor to pull the old one and set the new, haul-away and disposal, the permit, and — the part that ambushes shops — the code work. An expansion tank, a new T&P discharge, a drain pan, seismic straps, updated venting, sometimes a gas line that isn't up to spec. None of that is optional once you've opened it up, and all of it is easy to leave out of a from-memory quote.

"Install a water heater" isn't one job. It's a dozen jobs wearing the same name.

Don't copy the average. Build your own number.

That $1,350 average is a trap if you treat it as a target. It's a blend of shops doing it right and shops quietly undercharging, and if you aim at it you're aiming at their mistakes as much as their wins. Build yours from the ground up instead: your labor at your billable rate, your parts at your target margin, the code items in as line items rather than as things you eat. The shop quoting $1,100 for the same work is not cheaper; it is undercharging and calling it competitive. The code line that saves you

Put the surprises on the page before they happen: "Price assumes existing venting, gas line, and connections meet current code. Upgrades required on inspection are quoted separately." Now the day you find the undersized vent, you're pointing at a line you both already read — not eating it, and not arguing on the doorstep.

Price it once. Keep it. Pull it up.

Here's the move the sharpest shops make, and it's the whole reason a water heater should never be quoted from memory again. You price it right one time — rate, parts, margin, code items, cushion — and then you don't throw that work away. You keep it. The next water heater that comes up, the number's already built and sitting there, ready before you pull into the driveway. Do that across your regular calls — the water heater, the disposal, the capacitor, the flush, the toilet reset — and quoting stops being something you sweat at the table and becomes something you already have in your pocket. That's the difference between a shop that reprices the same job forever and one that priced it once, correctly, and moved on.

Questions

How much does it cost to install a water heater in 2026?

Nationally in 2026, a standard tank water heater replacement typically runs about $880 to $1,825 installed, averaging near $1,350. Tankless installs run higher, roughly $1,400 to $3,900, because of the extra gas, venting, and electrical work. The wide range comes down to unit type, code upgrades, and how hard the swap is.

How long does it take to install a water heater?

A straightforward tank swap is usually two to three hours of labor. Tankless conversions take much longer, often most of a day, because they add gas line, venting, and sometimes electrical work. Price the labor at your billable rate, not a flat guess.

Should I charge flat rate for a water heater install?

Yes, for a standard swap you can see the whole job, so a flat price wins more work and protects your margin. Price it once from your real rate, parts margin, and code items, then keep that number ready for the next one. Bill hourly only when there are true unknowns.
